## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

This study combined static features, ICU time series, and a transcriptomic pathway prior for ordinal discharge outcome prediction. The stacked ensemble achieved a macro-AUC of 0.830, compared with 0.826 for the strongest baseline, XGB-static+TS. The patient-level bootstrap 95% CI of the difference was +0.0005 to +0.0083, computed from five-fold OOF predictions. ICU time series improved reclassification over static features alone, with a mean category-free NRI of +0.19 across the five ordinal thresholds and IDI values of 0.002 to 0.060.

The temporal pathway atlas was reproducible across three statistical testing schemes. The primary analysis identified 70 subacute-phase pathways with significant paired activity change under BH-FDR control. Paired Wilcoxon and permutation-based FDR schemes recovered at least 62 of these 70 pathways, and no more than one of 6,306 acute-phase pathways was significant under any testing scheme, indicating that the acute-phase null result was qualitatively reproduced. These findings suggest that the 70-pathway set reflects consistent stroke-related remodeling rather than a single-test artifact.

The pathway prior, in contrast, contributed no meaningful predictive gain. Macro-AUC of DG-GRU+TPR across an 18-point regularization grid spanned 0.8140 to 0.8172, a range of 0.0032. Corrupted priors did not reduce performance: anti-TPR reached 0.8146, perm-TPR 0.8152, the intact prior 0.8147, and the unregularized baseline 0.8136. The default configuration showed a fold-averaged point estimate of ΔAUC +0.0011 and a bootstrap mean of +0.0009, with a 95% CI of −0.0006 to +0.0024, against the unregularized model. The pathway-prior increment also showed negative NRI at every threshold, with a mean of −0.28, consistent with this null. This null may reflect redundancy between the biological prior and clinical features, or a signal too weak to affect discrimination. We therefore position the pathway prior as an interpretability and structural-constraint mechanism, and we make no performance-improvement claim for it. Consistent with this positioning, the prior measurably reshaped the latent trajectory: compared with the unregularized DG-GRU, DG-GRU+TPR reduced the mean absolute step-to-step variation of the latent recovery score by 62.3% and produced a strictly outcome-ordered latent score, while discrimination was unchanged (Figure 2B).

### 4.2 Interpretation

Feature-group importance suggests that laboratory values and GCS carried most of the predictive signal in the gradient-boosted models, with gains of 0.48 and 0.19, respectively. This pattern is consistent with metabolic disturbance and neurological impairment dominating early stroke trajectories. Static summaries and time series encoded complementary information, because the ensemble exceeded either input view alone and the temporal increment persisted across four label definitions, from ΔAUC +0.006 to +0.010. The increment shrank under a strict time split to ΔAUC +0.002, which tempers the expected gain outside the training period.

The ordinal discharge outcome offers finer-grained stratification than a binary mortality endpoint. Reclassification gains were largest at the most severe threshold, reaching an NRI of 0.460 at in-hospital death. Discharge disposition is a feasible proxy because it is routinely recorded, available for essentially all admissions, and ordered by care dependence. Its construct validity is supported by triangulation against harder endpoints: the stacked ensemble discriminated in-hospital mortality and home discharge with AUROCs of 0.972 and 0.905, respectively, and the predicted expected outcome level increased monotonically with hospital length of stay and across the six outcome classes (Figure 2E).

External validation in eICU-CRD provided evidence for the generalizability of the gradient-boosted pipeline. Direct transfer of the MIMIC-trained XGB-static+TS model preserved ordinal discrimination, with a four-class macro-AUC of 0.809, and preserved mortality discrimination, with a mortality AUROC of 0.962 on the full eICU cohort. The modest gap relative to eICU internal cross-validation, macro-AUC 0.905, was consistent with the distribution-shift penalty expected for a model trained in a different health system. Calibration degraded under transfer and would require site-specific recalibration before the absolute risk estimates were used clinically. Direct transfer to MIMIC-III produced nearly identical discrimination, with a four-class macro-AUC of 0.814 and a mortality AUROC of 0.920, and performance was stable across the early and late admission eras. The deep models transferred to eICU with four-class macro-AUC values of 0.807 to 0.816 for all variants except the unregularized GRU, which decreased to 0.766, so the transfer evidence extends to the recurrent architectures rather than to the gradient-boosted pipeline alone. Together these analyses indicate that both the gradient-boosted pipeline and the deep architecture generalize beyond the development database.

The deep model alone underperformed the gradient-boosted baselines, with macro-AUC 0.815 versus 0.826. Its contribution to the ensemble was modest, a gain of ΔAUC +0.0044 that was stable across all five random seeds. The two model families agreed on 57% of top-1 decisions, and their predicted risk levels were highly correlated, with a Spearman rho of 0.95, indicating relatively limited complementarity.

### 4.3 Comparison with prior work

StrokeTHG reported that heterogeneous graph representations of EHR data improve multi-horizon stroke mortality prediction [60]. That framework targets binary survival endpoints and does not incorporate biological pathway knowledge. Our dynamic graph model instead applies a pathway-derived monotone-progression constraint to a latent recovery score and predicts an ordinal discharge outcome.

PathGNN applied pathway-topology graph neural networks to static gene-expression data for cancer survival stratification [61]. It showed that pathway structure can be embedded directly into the network, but it did not consume EHR time series or target ordinal outcomes. The present study transfers the pathway concept from static transcriptomic inputs to regularized temporal clinical modeling.

The proposed deep models were compared directly with GRU-D [36], a transformer encoder [37], and STGCN [38] on the same input schema. GRU-D reached a macro-AUC of 0.815, the transformer of 0.784, and STGCN-lite of 0.801, so the proposed models matched the strongest published recurrent baseline while providing the interpretable recovery trajectory.

Most EHR-based stroke prediction studies use static features and binary endpoints such as in-hospital or one-year mortality [3,4,5,62,63]. To our knowledge, no previous framework combined a cross-cohort pathway prior with ICU time-series deep learning for an ordinal discharge outcome. The present study addresses that gap, and it documents the boundary of the pathway prior's contribution at the same time.

### 4.4 Strengths

The pathway atlas was derived from four independent transcriptomic cohorts, and the pipeline was reproduced exactly, with a maximum absolute P-value deviation below 1e-15. All 70 atlas pathways remained significant after immune-composition adjustment: 70 of 70 after neutrophil-score residualization and 61 of 70 under a conservative ANCOVA intercept test. This robustness indicates that the 70-pathway set is reproducible within the derivation cohort rather than an artifact of the testing scheme or of white-blood-cell proportion shifts. Independent cohorts did not, however, show consistent directional concordance, and the set was not enriched for canonical stroke processes, so we treat the atlas as a derivation-specific biological constraint rather than a generalizable transcriptomic signature.

The performance estimates are supported by a full evidence chain. An information-leakage audit verified that training excluded outcome-derived columns, and a positive control that added discharge location as a feature reached macro-AUC 1.000, indicating the audit's sensitivity. Seed robustness, label robustness, and feature-window ablations agreed with the primary estimates, and temporal validation preserved the direction of the time-series gain. These analyses support the conclusion that the results are free of label and temporal leakage, and they indicate that the pathway-prior null is not an artifact of the evaluation design.

External validation is a further strength. The harmonized pipeline was transferred to two external databases, the multi-center eICU-CRD and the earlier MIMIC-III era, without retraining, and preserved discrimination in both. The deep models were transferred to eICU under the same schema with preserved discrimination, so the transportability evidence extends to the proposed architecture rather than only to the gradient-boosted baselines.

The 185-patient hospital cohort with ICU time series and recorded 90-day mRS outcomes closes the gap between the temporal pathway model and a true functional endpoint. Direct transfer of the deep temporal models reached a binary AUROC of 0.665, and fine-tuning the pretrained models on the hospital cohort recovered discrimination comparable to the static XGBoost transfer, AUROC 0.738 versus 0.739, while training from scratch on the same 185 cases reached only 0.542. The pattern suggests that the pretrained temporal representation transfers functional-outcome signal beyond what the small cohort contains alone.

### 4.5 Limitations

Several limitations bound the interpretation of these findings and should be weighed alongside the strengths.

First, MIMIC-IV does not record the modified Rankin Scale, abbreviated mRS. Discharge disposition captures the care setting rather than measured functional recovery. The ordinal discharge outcome is therefore a proxy, and the estimates may not transfer to mRS-based outcomes. The 185-patient hospital cohort with ICU time series and recorded 90-day mRS outcomes partially addresses this limitation, with direct transfer reaching a binary AUROC of 0.739 for the XGBoost model and 0.665 for DG-GRU+TPR, and fine-tuning recovering 0.738; the hospital cohort is single-center, and its mRS assessments were not blinded to the clinical course.

Second, the development cohort is single-center and retrospective, drawn from one health system. External validation in the multi-center eICU-CRD and in the earlier MIMIC-III era reduced this concern, but both analyses required collapsing the six-level outcome to four levels, the harmonized feature schema omitted database-specific variables, and MIMIC-III originates from the same institution as MIMIC-IV, so it does not provide full independence. Performance may still differ in other health systems and in other patient populations.

Third, the modeling cohort is an ICU subset of the stroke population. Time-series analyses were restricted to 2,875 of 4,858 stroke admissions with complete ICU sequences. Patients without ICU admission or with incomplete sequences were excluded, so the findings apply to ICU-admitted patients rather than the full stroke population.

Fourth, the biological prior is indirect relative to the clinical data. The temporal pathway atlas comes from peripheral blood transcriptomes, one step removed from the clinical trajectories it regularizes. The prior may therefore miss the biology most relevant to in-hospital recovery. The 70-pathway set, moreover, did not show consistent directional concordance in independent cohorts and was not enriched for canonical stroke processes, so its biological interpretation should be considered derivation-specific.

Fifth, calibration was not stable across time in the extrapolation analyses. Cross-time extrapolation produced significant calibration drift, with P<0.001, although discrimination remained directionally consistent. Time-aware isotonic recalibration reduced the drift at most thresholds while exactly preserving discrimination, but residual drift remained detectable, so periodic recalibration would be required before deployment. Absolute risk estimates from periods outside the training window should be interpreted cautiously.

Sixth, the statistical and clinical utility evidence is limited by the study design. Two classes were nominally significant in the DeLong test before correction, with P = 0.048 and P = 0.047, but no class remained significant after multiple-comparison correction, where the minimum adjusted P was 0.286. DCA showed only marginal net-benefit gains, concentrated at the highest-risk threshold. The ensemble advantage over XGB-static+TS was small, and no significance claim accompanies it.

Seventh, the value of the pathway prior is interpretational rather than predictive. Because its predictive contribution was negligible, its clinical value rests on the interpretable structure it imposes. The monotone-progression constraint yields a transparent recovery trajectory. Quantified sensitivity analyses support this positioning: the regularized latent score responded monotonically to in-silico physiological deterioration, the structural gains were reproducible across random seeds, and the latent score carried prognostic information earlier in the stay than the unregularized score. Two further limitations bound this interpretational claim. The 511-patient mRS cohort contains no ICU time series, so the deep model and its latent trajectory could not be evaluated against that functional endpoint; the 185-patient hospital cohort, which combines ICU time series with 90-day mRS outcomes, partially fills this gap, with direct transfer of the deep models reaching a binary AUROC of 0.665 and fine-tuning recovering 0.738. The 511-patient cohort validates only the admission-time risk signal, whose transfer AUROC of 0.635, stable across alternative outcome thresholds (0.602 to 0.635), indicates partial transportability with degraded calibration. The intervention-anchored trajectory analysis, in addition, found no significant shift in the latent score around intravenous alteplase or mechanical thrombectomy, and the point estimates were not stable across model seeds; the small analyzable sample and the day-level anchor for thrombectomy leave this analysis inconclusive rather than negative.

Eighth, the temporal pathway atlas is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The 70-pathway set was reproducible within its derivation cohort but showed low directional concordance in independent transcriptomic cohorts and was not enriched for canonical stroke processes, so its biological interpretation is derivation-specific. The clinical model does not depend on independent replication of the atlas, but the mechanistic interpretation of the regularizer should be read accordingly.

Ninth, the deep models were re-trained on the harmonized eight-channel schema for transfer rather than tested on their original 14-channel input. Their transferred discrimination in eICU was preserved, but the stacked ensemble itself was not transferred, because it combined model families across heterogeneous input views. The external validation therefore covers the deep architecture and the gradient-boosted pipeline separately, not the exact ensemble deployed internally.

### 4.6 Future

External validation in eICU-CRD and MIMIC-III is complete, and it indicated that the harmonized feature schema and ordinal prediction framework transfer across health systems and recording eras. Outcome-definition sensitivity with a combined discharge and 90-day mortality proxy preserved internal discrimination, indicating that the conclusions are not merely an artifact of the discharge-based label. The independent 511-patient cohort with recorded 90-day mRS outcomes provided partial evidence of transportability of the admission-time risk signal, with a transfer AUROC of 0.635 and an internal cross-validation AUROC of 0.739, but the deep temporal model could not be evaluated on that cohort because it contains no ICU time series; the 185-patient hospital cohort provided a first, single-center evaluation of the deep temporal model against a functional outcome. Further validation in larger cohorts that combine modified Rankin Scale labels with ICU time series, and in imaging-enriched datasets, would strengthen outcome measurement and clinical relevance, and would allow the latent recovery trajectory itself to be anchored to functional endpoints. The intervention-anchored analysis should be repeated in a larger, time-stamped recanalization cohort to determine whether the absence of a detectable latent-score shift reflects a true null or limited statistical power.

Stronger pathway-model coupling, such as attention over pathway membership or prior distributions over latent recovery states, might convert biological structure into predictive signal. Earlier attention-based variants overfit in our data, reaching macro-AUC 0.795 to 0.796, and were discarded. Any future coupling should preserve the regularization and interpretability that define the framework, and it should address the interpretability-gain tradeoff documented here.

## 5. Conclusion

This study integrated a hypothesis-generating temporal pathway atlas with ICU time-series deep learning to deliver an interpretable ordinal stroke recovery prediction framework. The stacked ensemble achieved the highest discrimination among all models, and robustness, negative-control, and leakage-audit analyses support the internal validity of the estimates. Discrimination transferred across two independent critical care databases, and the admission-time risk signal transferred partially to a true functional outcome in an independent cohort with recorded 90-day mRS. We position the pathway prior as an interpretability mechanism, because its structural constraints provide a transparent recovery trajectory despite a negligible predictive contribution, and we report the boundaries of this claim, including the absence of a detectable intervention-specific trajectory shift in a small exploratory analysis.
