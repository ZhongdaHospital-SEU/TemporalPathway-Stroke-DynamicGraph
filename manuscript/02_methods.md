## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study used four data sources. The first was the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV version 3.1 (MIMIC-IV), a public, de-identified critical care electronic health record database used for model development [19]. The second was MIMIC-III version 1.4 [20], an earlier critical care database from the same institution, used as an additional external validation cohort. The third was the eICU Collaborative Research Database version 2.0 (eICU-CRD) [21], a multi-center critical care database used for external validation. The fourth comprised four public blood transcriptomic stroke cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), namely GSE37587, GSE58294, GSE16561, and GSE22255. Access to MIMIC-IV, MIMIC-III, and eICU-CRD was obtained through credentialed PhysioNet training and certification. All data were fully de-identified, and the study was exempt from institutional review board approval. The study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement [22]. The overall study design, from the raw data sources and cohort flow through the transcriptomic pathway atlas to the prediction pipeline, is summarized in Figure 1A.

### 2.2 Cohort and outcome

The clinical cohort comprised 4,858 admissions with a primary or secondary stroke discharge diagnosis, identified by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes I60.x to I64.x in MIMIC-IV. The outcome was the ordinal discharge outcome, defined as the discharge disposition mapped to six ordered levels [23]. The levels were home (0), home care (1), inpatient rehabilitation (2), skilled nursing facility (3), hospice (4), and death (5). This scale is recorded routinely and serves as a pragmatic proxy of post-stroke recovery. Of the 4,858 admissions, 2,892 had an ICU stay with a usable time series. After requiring complete static features and outcome, 2,875 admissions formed the modeling cohort.

### 2.3 ICU time series

The ICU time series comprised 14 physiological and neurological channels extracted from the MIMIC-IV chartevents tables. The channels included the three Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) subscores (eye, verbal, and motor), seven vital signs, and four limb motor examination scores. Vital signs covered heart rate, respiratory rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, body temperature, and systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial blood pressure. Limb motor scores covered the left and right arm and the left and right leg.

Measurements were left-aligned at ICU admission and resampled onto a fixed hourly grid of 64 steps. After restriction to documented measurement windows, 69.5% of sequence cells were missing. Missing values were imputed with forward and backward carry within each channel, followed by mean imputation [24]. Each channel was standardized using means and standard deviations computed on the training fold only. A masking variable preserved the valid length of each sequence for the recurrent models.

### 2.4 Static features

A static feature set of 218 variables was assembled for each admission. The set included demographics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity), insurance and marital status, and comorbidity indicators. Admission laboratory values and vital signs contributed first, last, minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, and linear slope summaries. Continuous features were standardized using statistics computed on the training fold only. An augmented static feature set that additionally appended per-channel time-series summary statistics, matching the input view of the gradient-boosted time-series model, was used to test whether the deep model benefited from the same time-series information.

### 2.5 Temporal pathway atlas

We derived the temporal pathway atlas from four public blood transcriptomic stroke cohorts. GSE37587 contributed 34 patients with paired baseline and follow-up samples in the subacute phase. GSE58294 contributed cardioembolic stroke patients with blood samples at 3 h and 24 h after symptom onset in the acute phase. GSE16561 and GSE22255 contributed additional ischemic stroke transcriptomic profiles. These cohorts were selected because they provide paired longitudinal blood transcriptomes spanning the acute and subacute phases, which are required to define a temporal pathway prior. Probes were mapped to gene symbols with the platform annotation files, GPL6883 for GSE37587 and GSE16561 and GPL570 for GSE58294 and GSE22255, and averaged across probes mapping to the same gene; expression values were used as provided by GEO, and all temporal comparisons were made within cohorts, so no cross-cohort normalization was applied. Single-sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [25,26] produced activity scores for each sample across 6,392 curated gene sets from the KEGG, Reactome, and Gene Ontology biological process collections [27,28], using an exponent of 0.25 and a minimum overlap of five genes per set, with gene-set size filters of 5 to 5,000 genes for the KEGG and Reactome collections and 10 to 200 for the Gene Ontology biological process collection. Paired differential activity was tested with paired t-tests followed by Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate control (BH-FDR). The subacute comparison identified 70 significantly remodeled pathways at FDR < 0.05. The acute comparison identified none. These 70 pathways constituted the pathway prior for the deep model. We treat this atlas as hypothesis-generating: it motivates the direction and smoothness of the temporal pathway regularizer, while its cross-cohort biological generalization is tested explicitly and reported in Section 3.2 rather than assumed. Pathway-based deep learning has been used to embed biological structure into genomic models [29,30,31,32,33]. The atlas was further assessed under three testing schemes and after immune cell composition adjustment (Section 2.9). We also tested directional concordance of the atlas pathways in the independent cohorts and enrichment of stroke-related biological processes, as reported in Section 3.2.

### 2.6 Models

The prediction task was to assign each admission to one of the six ordinal discharge levels. Two gradient-boosted baselines were built with XGBoost [34]. XGB-static used the 218 static features only. XGB-static+TS used the static features together with aggregated summaries of the ICU time series. Both used 200 trees, maximum depth 4, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8, column subsample 0.8, histogram-based training, and a softmax objective over the six classes.

The recurrent baseline (SeqOrdinal GRU) encoded the ICU sequence with a single-layer gated recurrent unit of 64 hidden units [35]. Layer normalization and a 64-unit projection with dropout 0.2 followed the recurrent layer. An ordinal cumulative-link head used five thresholds and one latent recovery score to produce the six-class probabilities. The static features were concatenated to the head representation when available.

Three additional deep baselines were evaluated under the same cross-validation to benchmark the proposed architecture against published temporal models. GRU-D imputed missing values with input and hidden-state decays learned during training [36]. A transformer encoder with four attention heads and two layers [37] encoded the sequences with sinusoidal positional encoding. STGCN-lite combined two graph-convolution blocks with gated temporal convolutions over a fixed channel adjacency [38]. All three baselines used 64 hidden units, a 30-epoch schedule, and the same ordinal cumulative-link head as the recurrent baseline.

DG-GRU+TPR, the dynamic graph gated recurrent unit with temporal pathway regularization, extended the GRU with a per-step feature graph and a trajectory regularizer, building on graph neural network [16,39], dynamic graph [15,40], and heterogeneous graph [41,42] formulations. At each step, 16-dimensional edge embeddings computed from the current physiological state generated a time-varying adjacency among the 14 channels, following graph representation-learning paradigms [43,44]. A graph aggregation layer [45] updated the channel values before the recurrent update. The temporal pathway regularizer encoded the pathway prior as monotone progression and bounded drift of the latent recovery score:

`L_TPR = λ1 · r_mono + λ2 · r_drift`

r_mono penalized decreases in the recovery score across consecutive valid steps. r_drift penalized (s_last − s_first − δ)², where s_first and s_last were the first and last valid scores and δ = 0.05. Default weights were λ1 = 0.2 and λ2 = 0.05. Figure 1B summarizes the complete architecture, from the 64-step ICU sequence and the per-step dynamic feature graph through the pathway-regularized latent recovery score to the ordinal cumulative-link output, together with the level-1 logistic stacking ensemble. All recurrent models were trained for 30 epochs with cross-entropy loss on the six classes. Models were implemented in PyTorch [46] and scikit-learn [47]. Ablation variants included GRU, GRU+TPR, and DG-GRU, which isolated the contributions of the graph and the regularizer. Graph-based and similarity-based models have been widely applied to EHR risk prediction [48,49,50,51,52,53].

The final model was a stacking ensemble. Level-1 logistic regression (C = 0.1) combined the out-of-fold (OOF) probabilities of XGB-static, XGB-static+TS, and DG-GRU+TPR. Stacking was nested within the same five-fold structure, so the level-1 model was fitted on four folds and applied to the held-out fold.

### 2.7 Cross-validation and metrics

All models were evaluated with five-fold stratified cross-validation (random_state = 42). For each fold, a model was trained on four folds and predicted on the held-out fold, which yielded OOF predictions for the full modeling cohort. The primary metric was macro-AUC, the mean of the six one-versus-rest areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Accuracy, Cohen's kappa [54], Somers' D, and Brier score were reported as secondary metrics. Differences in macro-AUC between models were quantified with patient-level bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates) and reported as 95% percentile confidence intervals. Per-class AUC differences were tested with the DeLong test [55]. Multiple testing was controlled with Holm and Bonferroni corrections.

### 2.8 Clinical utility

Clinical utility was assessed with three analyses. Category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) [56] were computed for the ICU time-series increment (XGB-static+TS versus XGB-static) and for the pathway-prior increment (DG-GRU+TPR versus DG-GRU) at each threshold y ≥ k for k = 1 to 5 [57]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) [58] compared the net benefit of the ensemble with those of XGB-static+TS and the treat-all and treat-none strategies. Calibration of the predicted poor-outcome risk, P(y ≥ 3), was assessed with the calibration slope, intercept, and R².

### 2.9 Robustness, negative controls, and leakage audit

Ten additional analyses probed the robustness of the main results and the contribution of the pathway prior.

1. Regularization strength. An 18-point grid over the TPR weights λ1 and λ2 was trained, including the default (0.2, 0.05).

2. Negative controls. Anti-TPR reversed the monotonicity direction, and perm-TPR applied the prior to a randomly time-permuted trajectory.

3. Temporal validation. A temporal split (training through 2019, test 2020 to 2022) and expanding-window extrapolation were evaluated. Time-aware isotonic recalibration was applied to the temporal-split predictions to quantify the correctability of cross-time calibration drift. Feature-window length was ablated at 6, 24, and 48 h versus the full stay.

4. Label robustness. Three pairs of adjacent classes were merged (hospice with death, home care with rehabilitation, and home with home care), and the XGB-static versus XGB-static+TS comparison was repeated under all four label definitions.

5. Seed robustness. The XGB and stacking pipeline was repeated under five random seeds (1, 7, 42, 123, and 2024).

6. Pathway testing schemes. The atlas was re-derived with paired Wilcoxon tests and with a permutation-based empirical FDR (1,000 permutations), in addition to the paired t-test with BH-FDR.

7. Immune composition adjustment. The atlas pathways were re-tested after adjustment for immune cell composition, including neutrophil-adjusted, analysis-of-covariance, and seven-cell joint adjustments.

8. Statistical correction. Per-class DeLong P-values were corrected with Holm and Bonferroni procedures.

9. Leakage audit. Training scripts were audited for outcome-derived variables, and a positive control that added the discharge location as a feature verified the audit's sensitivity (macro-AUC = 1.000). ICU sequence timestamps were verified to be anchored at ICU admission, with no future information in the feature window.

10. Outcome-definition sensitivity. A combined proxy label defined a poor outcome as discharge to a skilled nursing facility, hospice, or death, or death within 90 days of admission, and the gradient-boosted pipeline and the deep model were re-evaluated under this label. In the mRS cohort, alternative good-outcome thresholds (mRS 0-1, 0-2, and 0-3) were tested for the transferred risk score.

### 2.10 External validation in eICU

eICU-CRD version 2.0 [21] is a multi-center critical care database covering 208 hospitals. Stroke admissions were identified by ICD-9 codes 430 to 438 or stroke-related diagnosis strings, yielding 12,874 admissions, of which 12,820 had a usable ICU time series. Both databases were mapped onto a harmonized feature schema of 173 variables: 19 laboratory tests with six summary statistics each, eight physiological channels with seven summary statistics each, and three demographic variables. ICU sequences were extracted and resampled onto the same 64-step hourly grid used for MIMIC-IV. Because eICU discharge locations cover only four of the six MIMIC-IV outcome levels, the ordinal outcome was collapsed to four levels: home (0), inpatient rehabilitation (1), skilled nursing or other facility (2), and death (3). Six-class MIMIC-IV probabilities were aggregated to four classes (P0, P1 + P2, P3, P4 + P5) for the transferred models. As a sensitivity analysis, the eICU-CRD cohort was re-derived under a narrow ischemic-stroke definition restricted to ICD-9 codes 433, 434, and 436, and the same harmonization and modeling pipeline was applied without further modification.

Two analyses were performed. First, direct transfer: XGB models trained on the full MIMIC-IV harmonized cohort were applied to eICU without retraining. Second, eICU internal five-fold cross-validation was performed with four-class models on the same harmonized features. Discrimination was measured with macro-AUC and with mortality AUROC on the full eICU cohort. Calibration of the transferred P(y ≥ 2) was assessed with the calibration slope and intercept. Hospital-level heterogeneity was quantified by per-hospital mortality AUROC among hospitals with at least 30 patients.

Deep-model transportability was tested separately under the same harmonized schema. The four recurrent models, GRU, GRU+TPR, DG-GRU, and DG-GRU+TPR, were re-trained on a harmonized eight-channel version of the MIMIC-IV cohort, in which the sequence was reduced to heart rate, respiratory rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial blood pressure, body temperature, and GCS, together with the harmonized static features. Five-fold stratified out-of-fold predictions on MIMIC-IV served as the reference, and the fold-trained models were then applied to eICU without retraining, with six-class probabilities aggregated to four classes as described above.

### 2.11 External validation in MIMIC-III

MIMIC-III version 1.4 [20] is a critical care database from the same institution as MIMIC-IV, with admissions recorded from 2001 to 2012. Stroke admissions were identified by ICD-9 codes 430 to 438, yielding 6,324 admissions, of which 6,172 had a usable ICU stay. Sequences and static features were extracted with the same harmonized schema used for the eICU analysis, and the MIMIC-IV-trained XGB models were applied to MIMIC-III without retraining. The six-level outcome was mapped to the same four-level ordinal scale as in eICU, and the ordinal-valid cohort comprised 5,536 admissions. To probe time-shift robustness, the cohort was split into an early era, 2001 to 2007, and a late era, 2008 to 2012. Discrimination was measured with the four-class macro-AUC and mortality AUROC, and calibration of the transferred P(y ≥ 2) was assessed with the calibration slope and intercept.

### 2.12 External validation with recorded 90-day modified Rankin Scale outcomes

To anchor the admission-time risk signal to a functional outcome, we used an independent cohort of 511 stroke patients with recorded 90-day modified Rankin Scale, abbreviated mRS, outcomes. The cohort comprised 363 patients with a good outcome, mRS 0 to 2, and 148 with a poor outcome, mRS 3 to 6. In addition to the admission-time features, imaging and laboratory fields were collected for all patients, including infarct volume, ASPECTS score, first INR, first C-reactive protein, first D-dimer, first systolic blood pressure, first glucose, and the prestroke mRS, to test whether enriched features improve internal discrimination. Six admission-time features were aligned with the MIMIC development features: age, sex, first-recorded GCS, first-recorded lactate, first-recorded white blood cell count, and admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score. Because the cohort contains no ICU time series, the deep temporal model cannot be applied directly; the deep-model external validation is carried by eICU-CRD and MIMIC-III, and this cohort validates the static admission-time risk signal against a true functional outcome. Three analyses were performed. First, a five-fold stratified internal cross-validation of an XGBoost ordinal model on the full feature set, reported as binary AUROC for mRS 0 to 2 versus 3 to 6 and as one-versus-rest ordinal macro-AUC. Second, an internal cross-validation restricted to the five features shared with the MIMIC transfer model, age, sex, GCS, lactate, and white blood cell count. Third, direct transfer: a binary XGBoost model trained on the MIMIC-IV shared features with the poor-discharge-outcome label, defined as ordinal outcome of at least 3, was applied to the mRS cohort without retraining, and discrimination, decile calibration, and calibration slope and intercept were reported. Subgroup AUROC values were computed within recanalization, age, NIHSS, and GCS strata.

### 2.13 External validation in a hospital cohort with ICU time series and 90-day mRS

To test the temporal pathway model against a recorded functional outcome, we used a second independent cohort of 185 stroke patients admitted to the intensive care unit, ICU, of a single tertiary hospital. Each patient had an hourly ICU time series of 64 h covering heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, temperature, and Glasgow Coma Scale, together with admission-time features including age, sex, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, GCS, first laboratory values, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale, mRS, outcome. The cohort included 129 patients with a good outcome, mRS 0 to 2, and 56 with a poor outcome, mRS 3 to 6. The cohort was collected retrospectively from de-identified records with ethics approval and a waiver of informed consent, as detailed in the Declarations.

The hospital cohort was harmonized to the same 8-channel, 64-step tensor schema and the same static feature schema used for MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and MIMIC-III, with temperature converted to Fahrenheit and glucose to milligrams per deciliter. Three analyses were performed. First, a five-fold stratified internal cross-validation of an XGBoost model on fifteen admission-time features including the NIHSS, reported as binary AUROC for mRS 0 to 2 versus 3 to 6 and as one-versus-rest ordinal macro-AUC, to quantify the signal available in the cohort. Second, direct transfer: the MIMIC-IV-trained XGBoost model on the shared harmonized features and the MIMIC-IV-trained DG-GRU+TPR and GRU+TPR models on the 64-step, 8-channel tensors were applied without retraining. Third, transfer learning: the five MIMIC-trained DG-GRU+TPR fold models were fine-tuned on four fifths of the hospital cohort and evaluated on the held-out fifth, and the same five-fold procedure was repeated with the pretrained backbone frozen and with training from scratch, to separate the contribution of the pretrained temporal representation from the information available in 185 cases alone.

### 2.14 Intervention-anchored latent trajectory analysis

To probe whether the latent recovery score shifts around acute recanalization therapy, we anchored the model's per-step latent trajectory to two interventions recorded in MIMIC-IV. Intravenous alteplase administrations were identified in the input events table, itemid 221319, and anchored with the exact infusion start and end times. Mechanical thrombectomy was identified from the procedure table, ICD-10-PCS codes 03CG3Z7 and 03CG3ZZ, and anchored with the procedure date, which is day-level and therefore coarser. For each treated admission, the pre-intervention window comprised the last 64 observations strictly before the intervention and the post-intervention window the first 64 observations strictly after it, with a minimum of three observations per window. The fixed DG-GRU+TPR model, with the temporal pathway prior and its seed-42 weights, was applied to each window, and the mean and final latent recovery scores were compared with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To control for the natural time course of the ICU stay, each treated admission was matched one-to-one, without replacement, to an untreated control admission on age within 8 years and first-recorded GCS within 3 points; the control's pre and post windows were placed at the same elapsed fraction of the ICU stay as the treated admission's anchor. Treated-control differences were tested with a one-sided Mann-Whitney U test. The analysis was repeated with the seed-7 and seed-123 model weights to assess stability. Given the small number of treated admissions and the day-level anchor for thrombectomy, this analysis is explicitly exploratory. The analysis was repeated with a relaxed minimum of two observations per window and with a separate combined intravenous alteplase with thrombectomy group.

### 2.15 Software and reproducibility

Analyses were performed in Python version 3.10 with PyTorch version 2.4.1, XGBoost version 3.2.0, scikit-learn, pandas, and NumPy; the ordinal deep models used the cumulative-link head with softplus-threshold parameterization. Sequencing-based pathway analyses used single-sample gene-set enrichment as implemented with an exponent of 0.25 and the gene-set collections described in Section 2.5. All random seeds, cross-validation schemes, and preprocessing steps are documented in the analysis scripts, which are available at https://github.com/wangzhipeng-1/TemporalPathway-Stroke-DynamicGraph. The mRS cohort calibration analysis was performed with logistic and isotonic recalibration fitted exclusively on the MIMIC development out-of-fold predictions, so that no mRS label informed the recalibration mapping. A complementary cohort-internal recalibration was additionally performed for the mRS cohort, in which the logistic recalibration mapping was refitted on the cohort itself under five-fold cross-validation, to quantify the removable fraction of the residual calibration offset.
