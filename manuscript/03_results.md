## 3. Results

### 3.1 Cohort characteristics

The cohort comprised 4,858 stroke admissions, of whom 55.7% were male and the mean age was 67.1 years. The ordinal discharge outcome was distributed as 1,088 home discharges, 22.4%; 832 home-care discharges, 17.1%; and 1,203 inpatient rehabilitation discharges, 24.8% (Table 1). The remaining admissions comprised 761 skilled nursing facility discharges (15.7%), 424 hospice discharges (8.7%), and 550 in-hospital deaths (11.3%). Mean age increased from 58.9 years among patients discharged home to 70.9 years among patients who died. Median first GCS declined from 15 to 8 across the same extremes, and ICU length of stay and the number of ICU observations were higher among patients with worse outcomes. The time-series modeling cohort comprised 2,875 patients with complete ICU sequences, static features, and outcomes (Figure 1A).

### 3.2 Temporal pathway atlas

Single-sample gene-set enrichment produced activity scores for 12,614 curated gene sets across the four transcriptomic cohorts. In the subacute phase of GSE37587, with 34 paired patients measured at baseline and follow-up, 70 pathways were significantly remodeled at BH-FDR < 0.05 (Table 2). These included down-regulation of dopaminergic neuron differentiation and up-regulation of nucleic-acid-templated transcription (Figure 1C). In the acute phase of GSE58294, comparing 3 h with 24 h, no pathway survived BH-FDR control (Figure 1D).

[[FIG:1C]]

[[FIG:1D]]

The 70-pathway atlas was reproduced under three testing schemes. Paired t-test with BH-FDR, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test with BH-FDR, and paired t-test with permutation-based FDR identified 70, 90, and 79 significant pathways, respectively. The Jaccard similarity between each scheme and the atlas was at least 0.63. The acute-phase null result was reproduced under all three schemes. All 70 atlas pathways remained significant after residualizing paired activity changes on neutrophil-score change, and 61 of 70 survived a conservative ANCOVA intercept test. Re-derivation reproduced each pathway P-value (maximum absolute deviation < 1e-15). As a further preprocessing sensitivity check, gene-level expression was re-derived from raw Affymetrix CEL files using RMA for the two Affymetrix cohorts, and ssGSEA activity was recomputed from these matrices. Pathway activity agreed closely between the platform-supplied and RMA-derived matrices in GSE22255 (median per-pathway Spearman rho = 1.000, n = 6,306 pathways) and remained concordant in GSE58294 (median rho = 0.698, interquartile range 0.532 to 0.832). In the acute-phase comparison of GSE58294, the null result was reproduced exactly: no pathway survived BH-FDR control in either derivation, the per-pathway t-statistics correlated at rho = 0.617, and 73.8% of pathway directions agreed. These checks indicate that the atlas conclusions do not depend on the preprocessing choice. We additionally tested whether the 70-pathway set generalized across cohorts. Directional concordance with the atlas was low in the independent cohorts, 22.9% in GSE16561 and 48.6% in GSE22255, and the paired acute-phase comparison in GSE58294 moved opposite to the atlas direction in 34.3% of the pathways (two-sided binomial P = 0.012). The atlas pathways were not enriched for canonical stroke processes such as inflammation, hypoxia, coagulation, or neurogenesis after multiple-testing correction (all false discovery rates = 1.00). These results indicate that the 70-pathway set reflects reproducible remodeling within the derivation cohort. We therefore position the atlas as hypothesis-generating: it provides a mechanism-motivated prior for the clinical model, while its cross-cohort biological generalization remains an open question that we test explicitly rather than assume. The atlas was used only to define the direction and smoothness constraints of the temporal pathway regularizer, and the clinical conclusions of this study do not depend on its independent replication.

### 3.3 Model performance

Table 3 reports five-fold out-of-fold (OOF) performance of all models. The stacked ensemble achieved the highest macro-AUC of 0.830, with an accuracy of 0.54 and Cohen's kappa of 0.41. XGB-static+TS reached a macro-AUC of 0.826 and XGB-static reached 0.818. The deep models ranged from 0.814 for GRU and DG-GRU to 0.815 for GRU+TPR and DG-GRU+TPR. Among the three additional deep baselines, GRU-D reached a macro-AUC of 0.815, STGCN-lite of 0.801, and the transformer of 0.784, with accuracies of 0.52, 0.50, and 0.47 and kappa values of 0.39, 0.37, and 0.35 (Table 3). GRU-D therefore matched the best single deep model, and the stacked ensemble remained the best-performing model overall. Providing the deep model with the same time-series summary features that improved the gradient-boosted model did not close the gap: DG-GRU+TPR trained on the augmented static feature set reached a macro-AUC of 0.811, compared with 0.815 on the original feature set (Table 3), suggesting that the temporal information in these summary features was already captured by the sequence encoder. The ensemble exceeded XGB-static+TS by ΔAUC +0.0044, with a bootstrap 95% CI from +0.0005 to +0.0083, and it exceeded every single deep model by ΔAUC from +0.0156 to +0.0465, with bootstrap 95% CIs excluding zero in all comparisons. Per-class DeLong tests showed nominally significant AUC increases for class 0, with dAUC +0.005 and P = 0.048, and for class 4, with dAUC +0.011 and P = 0.047. After Holm and Bonferroni correction across the six classes, the minimum adjusted P was 0.286, and no class-level difference remained significant (Table 4). These comparisons are therefore reported as point estimates with confidence intervals rather than as significant differences.

### 3.4 Contribution of the temporal pathway prior

Table 5 reports the contribution of the pathway prior within the deep models. Adding the prior increased macro-AUC by +0.0013 for the GRU, from 0.8139 to 0.8152, and by +0.0011 for the dynamic graph model, from 0.8136 to 0.8147. An 18-point regularization grid over λ1 × λ2 yielded macro-AUC values of 0.8140 to 0.8172, a range of 0.0032, with only 6 of 18 bootstrap confidence intervals excluding zero (Figure 2A).

[[FIG:2A]]

At the default configuration of λ1 = 0.2 and λ2 = 0.05, the prior contributed ΔAUC +0.0009, with a 95% CI from −0.0006 to +0.0024, relative to the unregularized DG-GRU.

Negative controls did not degrade performance. Reversing the monotonicity direction, anti, reached 0.8146; permuting pathway memberships, perm, reached 0.8152; and the intact prior reached 0.8147. These analyses indicate that the pathway prior provides interpretable structure rather than a clinically meaningful predictive gain. No performance-improvement claim is made for the pathway prior.

Beyond discrimination, the prior was associated with a measurable change in the internal representation. Compared with the unregularized DG-GRU, DG-GRU+TPR reduced the mean magnitude of monotonicity violations by 63.6%, from 0.0467 to 0.0170, and the mean absolute step-to-step variation of the latent recovery score by 62.3%, from 0.0910 to 0.0344, both with Wilcoxon P < 0.001 (Figure 2B).

[[FIG:2B]]

The mean net change in the latent score over the ICU stay reversed from −0.131 to +0.021, and the per-class mean latent score became strictly ordered across the six discharge outcomes, from 0.79 for home discharge to 1.50 for in-hospital death (Figure 2B). The regularized latent score was more strongly associated with the observed GCS course than the unregularized score, with pooled Spearman correlation of −0.69 versus −0.40, and mean total GCS declined monotonically with worsening outcome, from 14.6 among home discharges to 7.7 among in-hospital deaths. The pathway prior therefore produced the intended structure of monotone progression and bounded drift without improving discrimination.

Three further analyses characterize the interpretability of the regularized latent score rather than its predictive contribution. First, the latent score responded to clinically meaningful input perturbations in the expected direction. Decreasing the GCS components by three points in silico increased the mean final latent score by 0.260 (Wilcoxon P < 0.001), decreasing SpO2 by ten percentage points increased it by 0.213, and increasing heart rate by 20 beats per minute increased it by 0.045. A negative-control perturbation of temperature by plus or minus 0.1 degrees Fahrenheit shifted the score by only 0.010 in the direction of the perturbation, indicating that the score reacts to physiological deterioration rather than to arbitrary input noise. Second, the structural gains were reproducible across random seeds. Over three training seeds, the mean magnitude of monotonicity violations fell from 0.0572 to 0.0169 and the mean absolute drift fell from 0.850 to 0.343, with every seed showing the same direction of improvement and coefficients of variation of 0.055 and 0.230 for the regularized model. Third, the latent score carried prognostic information earlier in the stay under the prior: using only the first six sequence steps, the latent score alone reached a pairwise macro-AUC of 0.666 with the prior versus 0.610 without, whereas the full-stay latent scores were comparable (0.717 versus 0.734). The reversed-direction and time-permuted negative controls also reduced the violation magnitude and drift, as expected for any regularization that smooths the trajectory, but only the intact prior produced a positive mean net change in the latent score, indicating that the direction of the imposed structure derives from the pathway prior rather than from regularization alone. These results support positioning the pathway prior as a mechanism that yields an earlier, smoother, and clinically responsive recovery trajectory rather than a predictive gain.

### 3.5 Clinical utility

Table 6 reports reclassification (NRI/IDI) and decision-curve results. Adding ICU time series to the static model (XGB-static+TS versus XGB-static) improved reclassification of the ordinal discharge outcome at every threshold. Category-free NRI was 0.100 at threshold y ≥ 1, 0.095 at y ≥ 2, 0.131 at y ≥ 3, 0.177 at y ≥ 4, and 0.460 at y ≥ 5. IDI ranged from 0.002 to 0.061, and the mean NRI was +0.19.

The pathway-prior increment, DG-GRU+TPR versus DG-GRU, showed negative NRI at all thresholds, with a mean of −0.28. DCA showed a net-benefit gain of +0.0044 for the ensemble over XGB-static+TS at threshold y ≥ 5 and a threshold probability of 0.1 (Figure 2C). Differences at other thresholds were negligible. Calibration of predicted poor-outcome risk, defined as P(y ≥ 3), was good, with calibration slopes of 0.96 to 1.08 (Table 7) and R² ≥ 0.98 (Figure 2D).

[[FIG:2D]]

The ordinal predictions were triangulated against harder endpoints to support the construct validity of the discharge-disposition proxy. The stacked ensemble discriminated in-hospital mortality with an AUROC of 0.972 and home discharge with an AUROC of 0.905, and the predicted expected outcome level was positively correlated with hospital length of stay (Spearman rho = 0.417, P < 0.001; Figure 2E). Mean predicted expected level increased monotonically across the six outcome classes and across length-of-stay quintiles (Figure 2E).

### 3.6 Feature importance

XGBoost gain-based importance [59] was dominated by laboratory features, with a gain of 0.48, followed by GCS at 0.19, vital signs at 0.15, motor examination at 0.09, other features at 0.07, and demographics at 0.01 (Table 8; Figure 2F). GCS and laboratory features together accounted for 0.67 of total gain.

### 3.7 Robustness and information-leakage audit

*Temporal validation.* Under a strict time split, training through 2019 and testing on 2020 to 2022, XGB-static+TS achieved a macro-AUC of 0.811 versus 0.809 for XGB-static, with ΔAUC +0.002. Cross-time calibration drifted significantly, with P < 0.001, which we report as a limitation. Time-aware isotonic recalibration fitted on the training period reduced the drift at thresholds y ≥ 1 and y ≥ 2, for example the decile-based chi-squared statistic at y ≥ 2 fell from 43.6 to 26.4, while exactly preserving discrimination (tie-robust AUROC unchanged; Figure 2G). Residual drift remained detectable at some thresholds (P < 0.001), indicating that periodic recalibration would be required in deployment.

[[FIG:2G]]

*Feature-window ablation.* Models trained on the first 6, 24, and 48 h and on the full stay achieved macro-AUC values of 0.787, 0.795, 0.794, and 0.824, respectively. Performance increased with window length, and the 24 h and 48 h windows were nearly equivalent.

*Seed robustness.* Repeating the XGB and stacking pipeline under five seeds produced macro-AUC means of 0.827 ± 0.001 for XGB-static+TS and 0.831 ± 0.001 for Stack. The ensemble exceeded XGB-static+TS in 5 of 5 seeds.

*Label robustness.* Under four label definitions, the original six-level scale and three adjacent-class merges, XGB-static+TS exceeded XGB-static in every definition. The AUC gain ranged from +0.006 to +0.010.

*Information-leakage audit.* All training scripts dropped outcome-derived columns, including discharge location, the ordinal outcome, and the hospital-mortality flag. A positive control in which discharge location was added as a feature reached macro-AUC of 1.000, verifying the sensitivity of the audit. ICU sequence timestamps were anchored at ICU admission, with a median first-record offset of +0.07 h. Out-of-window rows were traced to 349 admissions with multiple ICU stays rather than to data contamination. These checks provide evidence against label or temporal leakage in the reported results.

### 3.8 External validation in eICU

External validation in eICU-CRD [21] used 12,820 stroke admissions with usable ICU sequences, of which 2,477 had valid ordinal outcomes after harmonization to four levels. Direct transfer of the MIMIC-trained XGB-static+TS model achieved a four-class macro-AUC of 0.809, comparable to the harmonized MIMIC out-of-fold estimate of 0.819 and 0.096 below the eICU internal cross-validation estimate of 0.905 (Figure 3A). XGB-static transferred at a macro-AUC of 0.732 versus 0.812 in eICU internal cross-validation. Mortality discrimination transferred more strongly than ordinal discrimination: the mortality AUROC of the transferred XGB-static+TS model was 0.962 on the full eICU cohort of 12,820 admissions with 964 deaths, and 0.942 on the ordinal-outcome subset, compared with 0.984 in eICU internal cross-validation (Figure 3B). Hospital-level discrimination was consistent across centers: among 73 hospitals with at least 30 patients, the median hospital-level mortality AUROC was 0.980, with a range from 0.769 to 1.000 (Figure 3C). Transfer degraded calibration at P(y ≥ 2), with a calibration slope of 1.297 and an intercept of 0.666, indicating that recalibration would be required before the transferred probabilities were used as absolute risk estimates (Figure 3D). These results indicate that the harmonized feature schema and the ordinal prediction framework generalize across health systems, with a modest distribution-shift penalty relative to internal validation.

### 3.9 External validation in MIMIC-III

Direct transfer of the MIMIC-IV-trained XGB-static+TS model to MIMIC-III reached a four-class macro-AUC of 0.814 on 5,536 ordinal-valid admissions, compared with 0.778 for XGB-static. Mortality discrimination transferred strongly, with a mortality AUROC of 0.920 on the full cohort of 6,172 admissions and 0.924 on the ordinal-valid subset. The time-series increment over the static model was preserved in transfer, with a four-class macro-AUC gain of 0.036 and a mortality AUROC gain of 0.057. Performance was stable across admission eras: the four-class macro-AUC was 0.812 in the early era from 2001 to 2007 and 0.815 in the late era from 2008 to 2012, and the mortality AUROC was 0.919 and 0.923, respectively. As in eICU, transfer degraded calibration, with a calibration slope of 0.709 and an intercept of −0.528 for the time-series model. The MIMIC-III results are therefore consistent with the eICU findings in an earlier recording era of the same institution and support the transportability of the harmonized pipeline.

### 3.10 External validation with recorded 90-day modified Rankin Scale outcomes

In the independent cohort of 511 patients with recorded 90-day mRS outcomes, the mRS distribution was 112 patients with mRS 0, 118 with mRS 1, 133 with mRS 2, 62 with mRS 3, 40 with mRS 4, 26 with mRS 5, and 20 with mRS 6, giving a good-outcome rate of 0.710 (Figure 4A). In five-fold internal cross-validation on the full admission-time feature set, the binary model discriminated good from poor 90-day outcome with an AUROC of 0.739 ± 0.042, and the ordinal model reached a one-versus-rest macro-AUC of 0.556; adding the imaging and laboratory features did not improve internal discrimination, with a binary AUROC of 0.700 ± 0.060 and an ordinal macro-AUC of 0.565 (Figure 4B). The shared feature set achieved an internal AUROC of 0.770 in MIMIC-IV. Direct transfer of the MIMIC-IV-trained model to the mRS cohort reached an AUROC of 0.635 (Figure 4C), which remained above chance but fell 0.135 below the MIMIC internal estimate. Calibration of the transferred probabilities was poor, with a calibration slope of 0.486 and an intercept of −1.254; predicted risk compressed the observed gradient, with observed poor-outcome rates rising from 0.157 in the lowest decile to 0.549 in the highest, against predicted rates of 0.234 and 0.904. Isotonic recalibration fitted on the MIMIC out-of-fold predictions and applied to the mRS cohort reduced the overcompression, increasing the calibration slope to 0.644, and discrimination was essentially unchanged, with an AUROC of 0.626; the mRS cohort remained overpredicted in the upper deciles, with a recalibrated prediction of 0.734 in the highest decile against an observed rate of 0.549 (Figure 4D). The residual offset indicates that the outcome-definition shift from discharge disposition to a 90-day functional outcome produces a calibration gap that is partially, but not fully, removable by recalibration. Cohort-internal five-fold cross-validated Platt scaling restored calibration with a slope of 0.954 and an intercept of −0.038, with AUROC essentially unchanged at 0.632, indicating that the residual offset reflects distribution shift rather than model misspecification. Subgroup discrimination was highest in untreated patients, AUROC 0.711, and in patients younger than 60 years, AUROC 0.626. Among recanalized patients, discrimination was higher for mechanical thrombectomy, AUROC 0.616, than for combined intravenous alteplase with thrombectomy, AUROC 0.587, and was unstable in the 57 patients receiving intravenous alteplase alone, AUROC 0.415. Discrimination was lowest in the NIHSS 6-15 stratum, AUROC 0.530. Discrimination was stable across alternative good-outcome thresholds: the transferred risk score achieved AUROC values of 0.610, 0.635, and 0.602 at the thresholds mRS 0-1, 0-2, and 0-3, and the score correlated with the full ordinal mRS (Spearman rho = 0.217, Kendall tau = 0.157; ordinal one-versus-rest AUROC 0.528). The admission-time risk signal therefore transfers only partially to a true functional outcome, and we interpret the mRS cohort as evidence of partial transportability of the risk signal rather than of the temporal pathway model, which could not be evaluated on this cohort because it contains no ICU time series.

### 3.11 Intervention-anchored latent trajectories

To test whether the latent recovery score responds to acute recanalization therapy, we anchored the fixed DG-GRU+TPR model to 19 admissions with intravenous alteplase and 343 admissions with mechanical thrombectomy. After requiring at least three observations in each window, 16 alteplase and 13 thrombectomy admissions remained analyzable. After intravenous alteplase, the mean latent recovery score increased from 1.240 to 1.260, a mean delta of +0.020 (median +0.070), with 62.5% of patients improving, but the paired difference was not significant, P = 0.433 (Figure 5A). After mechanical thrombectomy, the mean score increased from 1.057 to 1.191, a mean delta of +0.134 (median +0.191), with 76.9% of patients improving, but the paired difference was again not significant, P = 0.110 (Figure 5B). Among one-to-one time-matched untreated controls, the mean pre-to-post delta was +0.133, comparable to the treated delta of +0.020, and the treated-control difference was not significant, Mann-Whitney U P = 0.780 (Figure 5C). The direction of the point estimates was not stable across model seeds: the alteplase delta ranged from −0.087 to +0.020 and the thrombectomy delta from +0.005 to +0.161 across seeds 7, 42, and 123 (Figure 5D). We therefore report no intervention-specific shift in the latent recovery score; the small analyzable sample and the day-level anchor for thrombectomy preclude a definitive interpretation, and the analysis is exploratory. An expanded analysis with a relaxed two-observation window and a separate combined intravenous alteplase with thrombectomy group (n = 14) did not change this conclusion: the combined-therapy estimate showed no consistent positive shift across seeds (mean delta from −0.295 to +0.024, P = 0.017 to 0.952), and the alteplase and thrombectomy estimates remained non-significant in the expanded windows.

### 3.12 Outcome-definition sensitivity

Because discharge disposition is a proxy for functional outcome, we tested whether the conclusions depend on the label definition. A combined proxy label defined a poor outcome as discharge to a skilled nursing facility, hospice, or death, or death within 90 days of admission, reclassifying 93 of 2,875 patients (3.2%) relative to the discharge-based label. Internal cross-validation of the gradient-boosted static and time-series model preserved discrimination under the combined label, with a binary AUROC of 0.893 ± 0.007 versus 0.891 ± 0.014 for the discharge-based label. Retraining the deep sequence model under the combined label likewise preserved discrimination, with a binary AUROC of 0.817 ± 0.023 versus 0.806 ± 0.032 for the discharge-based label.
